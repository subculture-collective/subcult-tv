# Against Metrics

Measurement is necessary. Metrics are dangerous.

That is the whole game.

Measurement helps us understand reality. Metrics often reshape it. When a number becomes a target, behavior reorganizes around it. That is not a moral failing. It is just what incentives do.

If follower count determines legitimacy, growth strategies will prioritize visibility over depth. If engagement rate becomes the benchmark, content will optimize for reaction over substance.

Metrics compress complexity into a single axis. They make comparison easy and competition hard to avoid.

The problem is not that numbers exist. The problem is what they leave out.

---

Consider cultural value. It includes trust, coherence, historical memory, internal norms, creative experimentation. None of that fits neatly on a spreadsheet.

Yet platforms routinely present simplified dashboards: impressions, clicks, retention, conversion. These figures are not false. They are incomplete. And incomplete measures distort strategy.

Creators start reading flat metrics as flat meaning. Communities read swings in visibility as swings in worth.

> The metric becomes the narrative.

At Subculture Collective, we try to separate metric from measure.

A metric ranks you. A measure tells you whether the thing is working.

A metric says, "You grew by 12% this week."
A measure asks, "Did this growth match what you were actually trying to do?"

Most dashboards never ask what the work was for.

---

When growth becomes mandatory, sustainability erodes. The most emotionally charged content tends to outperform nuanced discussion. The metric rewards escalation. Over time, the culture bends toward whatever gets rewarded.

This is structural, not accidental.

Rejecting metrics entirely is not feasible. Organizations need feedback. The challenge is to design feedback systems that illuminate without squeezing people.

What would a non-extractive dashboard look like?

It might foreground trends over rankings and explain anomalies instead of waving them around. It would resist public leaderboards. It would treat numbers as diagnostic tools, not identity markers.

Most importantly, it would ask a question before presenting an answer: what are you trying to do here?

Without clarity of purpose, metrics become external arbiters of value.

Culture is not a KPI. It is a living system. Measurement should serve that system, not reshape it into something easier to sell.

When in doubt, ask whether a number increases understanding or just adds pressure. If it only does the second one, it is misaligned.

Not everything that can be counted should count.
